Terrain Pipeline Review

Ten stages, from a geodesic grid to an exported world, with every tuned constant listed where it acts. Then seven findings — all now closed. One was a real correctness bug, one was wrong in the review and corrected by measurement, and one turned out to overturn a claim the project had been relying on.

What the pipeline is

One command, ten stages, one archive.

scripts/generate_world.py runs everything end to end and writes world.npz plus a manifest. The grid is cached to disk and reused; everything else is derived from --n and --seed.

.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\generate_world.py --n 785 --seed 1 --out data/world01

The organising decision — and the one that explains most of the parameter values below — is that the world is specified by its cell size, not its radius. Holding cells at 100 m for every n makes the sphere 64.9 km, about the area of Croatia. That buys a hillslope domain (a cell is 0.0086 km², below the 0.01–1 km² channel head) at the cost of world size. Every length in the pipeline now falls into one of three classes, and putting one in the wrong class is silent:

  • cell  absolute; not rescaled, because cell size no longer varies with n
  • world  a fraction of the sphere; rescaled by PlateParams.for_radius and its equivalents
  • vertical  physical; never referred to the sphere at all

Two modules sit outside this pipeline and are worth naming so the map is complete: terrain/amplify.py (Stage 8, output-side resampling, used only by render_world.py) and terrain/dem.py (reads SRTM GeoTIFFs for validate_metrics.py). Neither runs during generation.

The stages

Algorithm, then the constants that steer it. Numbering is the project's own and reflects real execution order.

0

Grid

terrain/grid.py · build_goldberg

Class-I Goldberg tiling: cells are the dual of a frequency-n subdivided icosahedron, giving 10n²+2 cells — all hexagons but exactly 12 pentagons. Connectivity is built analytically from the subdivision lattice rather than by Delaunay triangulation, because within a face the adjacency is arithmetic and the 30 shared edges stitch for free.

Lattice points are placed by equal-arc subdivision (slerp from each corner to the opposite edge, averaged over three corners), not by linear interpolation and normalisation — the latter bunches cells toward face centres at σ/mean ≈ 13%. The slerp construction gives σ/mean 0.89% and max/min 1.16, constant from n=64 to n=785, so no relaxation pass is needed. Both relaxation schemes tried made it worse.

Tectonic crust

terrain/stage1.py · generate_crust_tectonic + terrain/plates.py

Crust thickness is generated, never elevation. Elevation falls out of Airy isostasy and sea level is an area-weighted quantile solved for a target land fraction, so the coastline is a consequence rather than a drawn line.

Crust type comes from warped 3D simplex noise independently of the plate layout — six of Earth's seven major plates carry both types, and assigning per plate gives every continent a Voronoi silhouette. Plates are laid over afterwards: Poisson-disk seeds, Lloyd-relaxed, partitioned by spherical Voronoi at domain-warped query positions (raw Voronoi boundaries are exact great-circle arcs, and orogens track boundaries, so ranges would run dead straight). Motion is a genuine Euler pole per plate — an axis and an angular rate — with velocity as the cross product.

Boundary type comes from decomposing relative motion into normal and tangential components. Distance to the nearest boundary is a KD-tree query, not graph relaxation: graph distance on a hex lattice is anisotropic by up to 21.8%, and since uplift is an exponential in that distance the error baked lattice-aligned ridges into the uplift field before a single erosion step ran. Stress is smoothed 8 passes along strike for the same reason — each boundary cell owns a wedge of interior, so raw scatter renders as hard stripes fanning out from the belt.

Uplift is a separate operator (derive_uplift) so uplift models can be swapped without regenerating plates. Its shape terms carry their own unnamed weights: collision 1.0, arc 0.6, rift shoulder 0.45, graben −0.35, transform 0.12; the sum is clipped to [−1, +1] before scaling. The lower bound is −1 rather than 0 deliberately — clipping at zero threw away rift subsidence, and the consequence surfaced two stages later as a world with essentially no endorheic lakes.

The uplift budget is spent, and that is the operative fact. Steady-state hilltop curvature is exactly U/D. At U=100 and D=0.02 that is 5×10⁻³ 1/m — 100 m of convex relief over a 200 m hillslope and a basal slope of 1.0, which is 45°, right at the angle of repose. Measured slopes on the shipped world are already p99 62.5°, max 83.7°. Any proposal to raise U for more relief multiplies every hillslope gradient by the same factor.

2–4

Landscape evolution, coupled with climate

terrain/coupled.py → terrain/stage2.py + terrain/climate.py

The realism engine. dz/dt = U − K·A^m·S^n + D·∇²z, solved by Braun & Willett's implicit method: process cells in topological order so each cell's receiver is already updated, and the update becomes a scalar solve. O(N), unconditionally stable, million-year timesteps. Setting dz/dt = 0 gives θ = m/n exactly, which is the end-to-end check — it only comes out right if solver, routing, spherical geometry and metrics all agree.

Stages 2, 3 and 4 are one loop, run three times: solve climate on the current topography, convert precipitation into a discharge weight, erode, repeat. The coupling runs through discharge, not through a fudged erodibility. Stream power is properly E = K·Q^m·S^n; drainage area is only a stand-in for Q under uniform rainfall, so replacing the accumulated quantity with area·P/mean(P) restores the real variable, needs no solver change, and degenerates exactly to the uncoupled run when precipitation is uniform.

Two solver subtleties carry most of the correctness. Cells the depression fill had to raise are marked in_lake and skipped by both the fluvial solve and the diffusion term — exempting either alone achieves nothing, because whichever is left fills the basin on its own. And uplift and subsidence take different paths: positive uplift goes through the clamped solver, subsidence is applied afterwards unclamped, because the clamp that stops water cutting a closed basin would also forbid a rift graben from ever dropping below its outlet.

Hillslope diffusion is a finite-volume Laplacian on the mesh, and it is explicit, so it must be sub-cycled: the stability limit tracks cell area and falls as the square of cell size — 239 Myr at 9.77 km cells, 25 kyr at 100 m, against a 0.5 Myr fluvial step. diffusion_substeps computes the count from the grid and sub-cycles only the cheap term.

A bisection solves for the scale directly, which is self-calibrating and needs no per-resolution tuning; the two earlier normalisations failed in opposite directions and both looked fine on the drainage metrics.

3

Climate (inside the loop, and again after reflood)

terrain/climate.py · compute_seasons

Four solves through the year, returned as one object whose annual mean and total are what everything downstream reads. Four samples rather than two because four evenly spaced points determine a two-harmonic year exactly — mean, annual and semiannual — which is what makes Köppen's twelve months an interpolation rather than an assumption. The samples are offset off the solstices, because a set containing both equinoxes contains the same climate twice: this model has no heat storage, so two dates with the same declination give bit-identical results.

Temperature is latitude insolation (with a true day-length term, so polar night exists and Köppen's D and E classes can turn on it), minus the lapse rate, minus continental cooling weighted by |sin lat|^1.5, plus a maritime anomaly. Precipitation is a steady-state advection–reaction fixed point rather than time-stepped parcels.

Ocean heat transport is the only non-zonal mechanism. Everything else is a function of where a cell is; the maritime anomaly is a function of where its air came from, which is the only thing that can tell two coasts at the same latitude apart. Wind is Ekman-deflected 45° over water and then projected off every land neighbour it still points into — that second step is what turns flow along a coast and gives a gyre its poleward western boundary, which is the whole Norway-against-Labrador effect. The zonal mean of the resulting anomaly is stripped before use, because Earth's shallow equator-to-pole gradient already contains its own ocean transport and adding a modelled one counts it twice.

Two limits are recorded in the module rather than papered over. There is no subtropical desert belt from the right cause — a surface-only model has no aloft, so it cannot represent air that arrives dry; the subsidence_floor surrogate puts the belt in the right place for the wrong reason. And the rainfall distribution is twice as wide as Earth's (log₁₀ sd 0.83 against ~0.45), which is not a convergence artifact and does not respond to any of the six knobs swept against it — though it does respond to a new source: river_evaporation takes log₁₀ sd from 2.146 to 0.681 at n=785, because the six knobs were all coefficients on transport that was already there.

5

Glacial erosion

terrain/glacial.py · erode

Mass balance is driven by the Stage 3 temperature field, not by an elevation-only ELA, so a cell glaciates for being high, for being far north, or both — a latitude-only ELA put ice on 1.2% of land and missed low-lying polar shields entirely. Ice routes down the existing flow graph and erodes as a power law on flux.

The one thing that makes glacial terrain look glacial is that ice can cut below its own outlet. Water cannot, so the fluvial solver forbids it. Getting that to happen took a non-obvious change: simply removing the clamp achieves nothing, because with E ∝ S erosion vanishes exactly as a cell approaches its receiver's elevation and the bed asymptotes flat. The driving slope has to be regional, measured over a long flow path — which is also physically right, since ice responds to surface slope, not bed slope.

5b

Reflood, then climate on the new coastline

scripts/generate_world.py · stage1.reflood → climate.compute_seasons

The land mask is not a constant of the pipeline. Glacial erosion cuts below its own outlet — that is what carves a fjord — and the water then has to be let in, or those are dry fjords. Reflood re-applies Stage 1's two rules to the eroded surface: below the datum is water, and ocean is the largest connected body of it. Whatever is below the datum and cannot reach the sea stays land and becomes a Stage 6 lake candidate.

Climate is then re-solved on the coastline that actually ships, because coast distance, the ocean moisture source and the sea-surface anomaly all move when a fifth of the land floods. This does not feed back into erosion — Stages 2 and 5 have already run, so it is the reported climate being made consistent, not the landscape. Stage 5 therefore still sees the pre-reflood temperature, which is correct: that is the coastline the ice grew on.

6

Hydrology — lakes, endorheic basins, rivers

terrain/hydrology.py · analyse

Depression filling inside the erosion loop is a numerical device. This is where depressions become geography. The distinction that matters is exorheic versus endorheic, and it needs a water balance rather than geometry, which is why this stage comes after climate. Each depression's cells are sorted by bed elevation and the water raised until lake-surface evaporation equals catchment inflow; if that level lands below the spill point, the lake is terminal.

Runoff uses Turc–Pike rather than min(P, PET). The obvious form has a kink at P = PET and hands back exactly zero runoff everywhere drier — measured, that was 59% of all land, so the subtropics carried no rivers at all rather than thin ones.

Metrics and export

terrain/metrics.py + terrain/export.py

One artifact: field arrays plus n, radius and seed, since the grid is fully regenerable from n alone. The manifest records parameters and measured metrics next to the data — a world whose provenance is lost is not reproducible even when the generator is deterministic.

Findings

Seven, ordered by what they cost. One is a verified correctness bug with a one-line fix; three are documentation that has drifted out of agreement with the code; one is dead weight; two are scope questions rather than defects.

F1

Priority-flood's +ε is lost to float32 above 2,048 m, and uplift silently stops

correctness · verified

flow.py:23 sets EPS = 1e-4 metres, and the fill runs in float32. Float32 spacing at 2,048 m is 2.44×10⁻⁴, so filled[j] = key + EPS rounds back to key and the increment vanishes. Every cell inside a depression whose spill level sits above that elevation ends up on an exactly flat filled surface, and compute_receivers requires a strictly positive slope — so none of them drains. They all become self-receivers.

Three consequences, in descending severity. solve_stream_power tests r == i before it tests in_lake, so those cells take the out[i] = z[i] branch and uplift is never applied to them. flow_accumulate stops carrying drainage through the basin, so the trunk river below it loses that whole catchment. And glacial.abrade skips them on the same test.

Verified directly on the cached n=64 grid, routing a rough surface offset to different elevations:

offset      0 m:  spurious self-receivers      0   (of 537 lifted cells)
offset    500 m:  spurious self-receivers      0
offset  2,400 m:  spurious self-receivers    537   ← all of them
offset  5,000 m:  spurious self-receivers    537

Production peaks are 2,428 m, so ground above the threshold exists at n=785. Whether a closed basin currently sits up there is world- and seed-dependent — but the mechanism is unconditional once one does, and it fails silently: build_order still covers every cell, so the existing assertion does not fire. Note the related raised and in_lake tests use 1e-3, which survives to well past 8 km, so lake detection is unaffected — that is why the symptom is a missing forcing rather than a visible crash.

The fix is to make the increment scale with magnitude, e.g. key + max(EPS, |key| × 1.2e-7), which stays in float32 and costs one comparison in the inner loop. Moving the fill to float64 also works and costs ~25 MB at n=785.

FIXED. flow.py now takes max(EPS, |key| × 1.2e-7) — one float32 ulp relative, so the increment always moves at least one representable step. The repro goes 537 spurious outlets → 0 at every offset, and drainage accumulation is restored. Guarded by a new parametrised test at 0 / 500 / 3,000 / 9,000 m, which fails at HEAD on the last two.
F2

One uplift length never got rescaled with the sphere

consistency · verified

plates.derive_uplift computes the transform-boundary term as 0.12 × exp(−d / 100.0). That 100 km is a bare literal in the expression, so PlateParams.for_radius never sees it — while its four siblings (orogen_decay_km, arc_offset_km, arc_width_km, rift_shoulder_km) were all divided by 98.125.

On a 64.9 km world the uplift reach is 12.2 km, over which exp(−d/100) runs from 1.000 to 0.885. The term is therefore flat: transform domains receive a near-uniform slab of up to 12 m/Myr — six times the cratonic floor — instead of a ridge that hugs the boundary. Magnitude is modest, but the shape is wrong, and shape is precisely what stage2.uplift_field's own docstring argues matters more than magnitude.

This is the same class of bug the rescale already caught twice (the 1,200 km boundary reach, the 1000 km² island threshold). It survived because it is a literal inside an expression rather than a named field on the dataclass — which is itself the lesson worth recording.

This one had teeth. It is a real terrain change, not a cosmetic one: transform domains are 17.6% of land, and fixing the decay takes them off the slab and down to the cratonic floor beyond ~2 km. Measured at n=256, 2 × 200 steps, seed 1:

F3

Three module docstrings still describe the pre-rescale world

These matter more than ordinary staleness, because each is the place a future decision would go looking for its answer.

stage1.py:1–7 announces itself as "Stage 1, throwaway version" and says plates, Euler-pole motion and tectonic thickening are "none of that is here" — but the file's primary entry point generate_crust_tectonic is the full version, and it is what the pipeline calls. generate_crust is the throwaway one and is now only used by tests.

A fourth stale line in glacial.py — overdeepening counts quoted at 30 km spacing — is now marked as not re-measured since the rescale.
F4

margin_class is computed on every run and consumed by nothing

dead output

classify_margins runs inside generate_crust_tectonic, is carried on TectonicCrust, and is read only by a diagnostic print in run_stage2.py and one test. It is not passed to export_world, so it never reaches the archive. Its docstring promises it "drives shelf width, whether rivers end in deltas or plunge straight in, and where fjords versus barrier islands belong" — none of which is implemented anywhere.

Its active_within_km=400.0 parameter is separately dead: boundary_dist_km is capped at 0.188 R = 12.2 km with a 122 km sentinel for beyond-cap cells, so the close test is always true. The function still behaves sensibly (beyond-cap cells have nearest_boundary = −1 and so read as NONE), but the parameter reads as a tuning knob and is not one.

Two honest options: add it to the export, where it is cheap and genuinely useful to anything drawing coasts; or delete it and the print. Leaving it as-is is the only bad option, because it looks like a live feature.

Shipping the Stage 1 margins next to the post-reflood land mask would have exported a margin for a coast that no longer exists, which is the exact failure the reflood comment warns about. F5

Two constants stayed on Earth's vertical scale while everything else came down

scope

Neither is a bug. Both are the accounting gap the last commit named — elevation is an emergent field, and the rescale's three-class table covers parameters, not fields.

biomes.snowline_m puts permanent snow at 4,900 m at the equator. With peaks at 2,428 m, the elevation branch of the ice test cannot fire until roughly 55° latitude, so permanent ice is effectively a temperature band rather than a mountain feature — the thing the function exists to add.

glacial.max_incision_per_step_m = 8.0 over 60 steps allows 480 m of cutting against a pre-glacial relief budget of ~432 m. The clamp sits above the quantity it bounds, so it never binds and is not doing the safety job its name implies.

Measuring the second one showed the review was wrong about it. The clamp is not vestigial: on an n=128 world 7,417 land cells hit it, and the deepest total incision comes out at exactly 8.0 × 60 = 480.00 m — meaning the fastest-eroding cells are clamped on every single step. Wherever that holds, the local erosion rate is set by the clamp rather than by K·Q^m·S^n, so K and max_incision_per_step_m are not independent knobs and the twentyfold K sweep in GlacialParams does not describe the model on its own. That is a more useful fact than the one the review claimed.

The snowline claim held and is stronger than stated: land cells above their own snowline number zero. Every ICE cell comes from the −12 °C floor. A 2,428 m production peak clears the snowline only poleward of 49.8°; the n=128 fixture's 347 m peak needs 79°.

DONE — one claim corrected. Both documented where they sit. The snowline note explains why scaling it down would be worse than leaving it dead (it would paint ice on ground 300 m up and 10 °C above freezing; the temperature field is the honest authority and is already consulted). Neither constant was retuned.
F6

Mass wasting became resolvable at 100 m cells and is still absent

gap · already recorded

terrain-algorithms.md:278 already flags this, so it is confirmation rather than discovery — but it deserves its rank. At 100 m cells the angle of repose is 64.9 m of drop between neighbours. The shipped world measures p99 62.5°, p99.9 76.1°, max 83.7°: a real share of steep ground sits past repose and stays there, because nothing in the pipeline removes it.

The connection worth drawing is to the uplift ceiling. The reason u_max_m_per_myr is documented as a spent budget is that hillslope gradients are already past repose — but they are past repose partly because no operator relieves them. A talus term would not create relief on its own, and it is not a route to the lapse-rate shortfall; what it would do is make the slope distribution physically admissible, which is currently the pipeline's clearest departure from real terrain.

Built — and it does almost nothing, which is the finding. stage2.mass_wasting relaxes any edge past repose, moving material from the high cell to the low one with a volume split that conserves mass exactly on an unstructured mesh. Four tests pin it: mass conservation, the slope cap, that gentle ground is untouched, and that it never overshoots into an inverted slope. Then the sweep at n=256 came back flat — every repose angle from 45° down to 30° produced the same world (θ 0.438–0.463, peak 691 m, z std 47.4 m).

Decomposing the slope statistic explains why:

steep edges (>33°), n=128 after Stage 2countshare
touching an OCEAN cell169,07898.1%
touching a LAKE cell3,1241.8%
land–land, neither800.05%

Almost every over-steep edge is a cell against a surface pinned at a level — the shoreline, where ocean is held at base level, or a lake rim. Those are not hillslopes, and the operator correctly leaves them alone. Genuine hillslopes on the same world run p50 0.9° and p90 9.4°: nowhere near repose, because U/D equilibrium does not put them there.

This overturns a claim the project was relying on. plates.py argued the uplift budget is spent partly on the grounds that "measured slope distribution … p99 62.5° … so the steep ground is already past repose". Those percentiles are real; that reading of them is not. It is a statistic about the coastline, not about hillslopes. The conclusion survives untouched — it rests on C = U/D and the 45° basal gradient that implies, which is arithmetic — but one of its two supports was never load-bearing, and plates.py now says so.

Kept on regardless, at a cost that does not show in the run time, because it is a physical constraint that ought to hold and there is one regime where it bites: glacial erosion takes the land–land count from 80 to 4,530. Stage 5b then floods almost all of it (back to 38), so a post-glacial pass would relax ground about to become seabed — a property of this world's hypsometry, not a general one, and recorded as such.

DONE. Operator built, tested, swept, and defaulted on at 33°. The negative result and the plates.py correction are the real output.
F7

Checked and not worth doing: caching the reverse-slot table

measured · declined

climate._reverse_slots is a pure function of grid.neighbours and is rebuilt inside sst_anomaly, maritime_anomaly and precipitation — three times per season, four seasons per climate solve, five solves per production run. Sixty rebuilds of an identical table looked like an obvious cache.

Measured at n=256 it takes 7 ms, which extrapolates to 68 ms at n=785 and 4.1 s across all sixty rebuilds — 0.4% of a 1,070 s run. Not worth the field on Grid or the cache-invalidation surface. Recorded so it does not get re-proposed.

Leave alone

  • The rainfall distribution. Twice Earth's width, six knobs swept against it, none of them moved it. It is a single-layer model's limit, not a mistuning.
  • The subtropical desert belt. Right place, wrong reason, and fixing it properly needs a second vertical level.

The cost is Köppen Af at 18.1% of land against Earth's ~5%.

One thing worth knowing about the review itself

The pipeline is unusually well documented in-place: most constants carry the measurement that chose them, several carry the sweep table, and a few carry a negative result recorded rather than quietly dropped. That is why this review found what it did — F1, F2 and F4 are all cases where a value escaped the documentation habit by being a bare literal in an expression rather than a named field with a comment. That pattern is the one worth watching.

The fixes turned up a matching lesson about measurement, and it applies to this review as much as to the code. Three of the six items were wrong or misleading as first written, and in every case the correction came from measuring rather than reasoning:

  • F2 appeared to destroy the concavity metric. It did not — the fixture that said so was too small to resolve θ at a production channel threshold. A review that trusted it would have rejected a correct fix.
  • F5 claimed the glacial incision clamp never binds. It binds on 7,417 cells and caps the deepest incision at exactly 480.00 m. That claim was simply wrong.
  • F6 claimed a real share of hillslope sits past repose. 98.1% of it is coastline, and the operator built to fix it does almost nothing — which is worth more than the fix would have been, because the same statistic was propping up the uplift ceiling.

The pattern is that a slope percentile, a relief budget and a concavity fit all read as hard numbers, and all three were being asked a question they do not answer. The project's own habit — every constant carries the measurement that chose it — is what made the errors cheap to find, and it is the habit worth keeping.
